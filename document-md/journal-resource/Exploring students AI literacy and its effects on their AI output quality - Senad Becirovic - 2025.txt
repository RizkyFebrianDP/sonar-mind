Introduction
The availability and quality of AI-powered tools have increased dramatically, and these 
technologies are being used in educational contexts more and more (Chu et al., 2022; 
Ou, 2024). Students are experiencing the unprecedented impact of AI tools on their 
educational processes, performance and achievement. These changes also affect stu-
dents’ overall development, including personal, cognitive and moral dimensions. Thus,

increased AI-literacy, including competent and responsible usage of AI-tools and an 
increased focus on information literacy (Xiao et  al., 2025), is crucial for students to 
gain and maintain a competitive edge in the ever-growing digital landscape (Brew et al., 
2023).

Previous studies present conflicting findings regarding the relationship between AI-
powered tool usage and academic achievement. Some studies have shown that reliance 
on AI-powered tools impacts student performance negatively (Khoso et al., 2023; Wecks 
et al., 2024). However, other studies have pointed out that well-founded AI tool usage 
supports students’ performance and motivation (Montenegro-Rueda et al., 2023). Higher 
AI-literacy has been found to be connected to increased engagement with AI tools for 
learning purposes, which, in turn, leads to better learning outcomes and improved aca-
demic performance (Singh et al., 2024; Soni, 2024), leading to more student satisfaction 
due to individualised AI-supported adaptive learning (Shete et  al., 2024). This means 
that AI-powered tools can offer support to students in a wide variety of areas ranging 
from simple information retrieval to the provision of interactive exchanges that allow 
for individual and personalised learning experiences, as well as give support in fields as 
diverse as research, writing and designing (Aggarwal et al., 2023; Akgun & Greenhow, 
2022; Bećirović & Mattoš, 2024; Ou, 2024; Parsakia, 2023).

Naturally, all these advantages are closely tied to potential disadvantages. A decline 
in creativity, critical thinking and problem-solving abilities has been pointed out due to 
an overreliance on AI tools. Equally, increases in and the facilitation of plagiarism and 
cheating are major concerns. In addition, concerns have been voiced related to the loss 
of human interaction between teachers and students as a result of AI technology usage 
(Ou, 2024). Clearly, these issues pose major challenges for educational policy makers 
and administration, instructors, researchers and others in the field of education (Brew 
et al., 2023).

While students generally tend to have very distinct AI awareness within the domain 
of private use and daily life, there is room for improvement in the academic sector. This 
calls for a more holistic approach to AI-literacy development and a balanced integra-
tion of AI in educational settings (Fazil et al., 2024), which is the research gap this paper 
seeks to fill. Currently there is a limited amount of research pertaining to AI literacy and 
AI technology usage and acceptance in educational settings in Austria, which makes it 
difficult to adjust policies and adapt curricula to match the ongoing development of AI 
and provide students with the training they need to employ AI tools responsibly and 
successfully. Yet, it has been found that students have been using ChatGPT as a virtual 
tutor who does not pass judgment (Brew et  al., 2023), to explain content (Alsobeh & 
Woodward, 2023), prepare for exams, generate presentations and homework assign-
ments (Khoso et al., 2023), but also to cheat in exams. Further, AI tools may be used to 
generate translations (Brew et al., 2023) and support problem-solving and the streamlin-
ing of processes (PricewaterhouseCoopers, 2023a, 2023b). A recent Austrian study on 
AI acceptance in education (Brandhofer & Tengler, 2024) also found that almost 50% of 
student teachers and 75% of teachers rate the opportunities of using AI higher than the 
potential risks. This study also shows that 49.7% of participants expect AI applications 
to enhance their ability to reach their learning goals, which is demonstrated by survey 
(Krone, 2024) reporting that 73% of Austrian students feel generally competent in AI

usage in 2024 – an increase of 22% compared to 2023. However, Brandlhofer and Ten-
gler (2024) also found that, while a large number of students is enthusiastic about AI’s 
ability to support their learning, more than 45% stated that they distrust the output of AI 
applications. This is underlined by the fact that in Austria the application and integration 
of AI tools in educational processes are still regarded with skepticism. 80% of Austrians 
are deeply concerned about future AI-related developments, especially regarding gen-
erative AI tools such as ChatGPT (PricewaterhouseCoopers, 2024).

These contradictory perspectives highlight the need for more thorough and detailed 
inquiry and analyses of AI literacy and its influence on academic achievement. More-
over, further research is required to elaborate on the contradictory findings regarding 
the beneficial or detrimental effects of AI-powered tools on students’ academic perfor-
mance. Additionally, it is necessary to identify strategies for mitigating the misuse and 
negative consequences of AI-powered technologies use, while simultaneously enhancing 
their positive impact on students’ academic achievement and overall development.

AI is a swiftly advancing technology that continually introduces new advancements 
to worldwide education and other systems. Staying informed about the most recent 
advancements and having the ability to effectively and confidently utilise AI technol-
ogy have become essential skills in students’ daily lives. Therefore, the purpose of this 
quantitative study is to examine students’ AI literacy, including AI technological under-
standing, its practical application, and critical appraisal and their effects on students’ AI 
output quality, AI self-efficacy, and academic performance. Moreover, this study exam-
ines the effects of students’ AI self-efficacy and AI output quality on their academic 
achievement.

In addition to shedding light on the effects of AI-powered technology use on stu-
dents’ learning outcomes, filling the gaps, and contributing to professional literature in 
this relatively new and emerging academic area, this study may deepen knowledge and 
understanding of AI literacy among AI non-expert university students studying social 
sciences. Furthermore, gaining a more profound comprehension of the aforementioned 
relationships can aid in the implementation of successful approaches to effectively incor-
porate AI-powered technologies into educational processes. Such integration can assist 
students in enhancing their AI literacy and proficiency in utilising AI competently and 
efficiently, while also enabling them to employ these technologies ethically and responsi-
bly. Similarly, the findings of this study may aid in revising existing and designing new AI 
policies, curricula, and teaching approaches.

Literature review
The number of studies conducted on AI has seen a significant increase across the globe 
in recent years (Potter et al., 2024). People will need to have the ability to interact inde-
pendently and knowledgeably with AI (Alsobeh & Woodward, 2023; André, 2021; Car-
olus et  al., 2023; Ng et  al., 2021b). Thus, AI literacy is a crucial factor not only in the 
rapidly developing globalized world but also in the field of education as more and more 
research is conducted on this topic (Crompton & Burke, 2023; Pinzolits, 2023). Students 
require the skills to understand, employ and evaluate AI tools to gain maximum advan-
tage when using them (Bozkurt, 2023).



Bećirović et al. Smart Learning Environments           (2025) 12:29 Page 4 of 25

Ng et  al. (2021b) come to define AI literacy as a set of “different types of ‘literacies’ 
which have […] been applied to define skill sets in varied disciplines” (p. 507). These 
skills encompass “know and understand AI”, “apply AI”, “evaluate and create AI” and “AI 
ethics”, which overlap with the factors investigated in this research (Ng et  al., 2021b). 
Numerous studies on AI literacy (Cetindamar et al., 2024; Ng et al., 2021b; Wang et al., 
2023; Zhang et al., 2023) have been conducted and a number of competence fields have 
emerged, in addition and expansion to the ones found by Ng et al. (2021b) mentioned 
above. Technological understanding, practical application, critical appraisal, self-efficacy 
and output quality related to AI technology are all relevant and critical factors (Carolus 
et al., 2023) and form the core of this research.

This study’s research model comprises six factors (Fig.  1). It is based on Laupichler 
et  al.’s (2023a) framework of assessing non-experts’ AI literacy, which includes three 
factors: technical understanding, critical appraisal, and practical application of AI. 
The framework of assessing non-experts’ AI literacy ultimately suits the research sam-
ple of this study, which is social science students who are not experts in information 
and AI technologies. The research model of this study measures how these three fac-
tors, as main predictive variables, affect students’ AI self-efficacy, founded on (Carolus 
et al., 2023) Meta AI literacy, which assesses students’ problem-solving and learning by 
using AI tools. Furthermore, the research model evaluates how the three factors men-
tioned above influence students’ AI output quality, derived from TAM3 (Venkatesh & 
Bala, 2008) and, students’ academic achievement. Additionally, the model examines the 
effects of students’ AI self-efficacy and their AI output quality on students’ academic 
achievement.

Ultimately, the model evaluates the effects of students’ AI technical understand-
ing, their AI critical appraisal, and AI practical application, as well as AI self-efficacy, 
AI output quality, and students’ academic performance. The justification for including 
each variable in the current research model, as well as the hypothesized relationships 
between them, is described in the following sections.

AI technological understanding
Students have become aware of the potential of AI tools in educational settings and are 
interested in expanding their knowledge in this area (Daher et al., 2024; Mohamed et al., 
2024). They need to “know the basic functions of AI and how to use AI applications in

everyday life ethically” (Ng et  al., 2021a, p. 507), if they want to employ this technol-
ogy successfully. Recent studies showed that technological awareness of AI seems to be 
relatively equally distributed among age groups and genders, showing a medium level of 
self-perceived competence (Asirit & Hua, 2023; Kamoun et al., 2024). However, diverg-
ing levels of technological awareness by field of study were found (Bećirović, 2023a). 
Students in programs related to education and criminology showed higher levels of 
awareness of AI tools and their potential than students studying in business programs 
(Asirit & Hua, 2023), while management students outperformed engineering students 
(Kamoun et al., 2024). Recent findings support the shift in AI technology competence 
from engineering to other fields (Chu et al., 2022; Zawacki-Richter et al., 2019) where 
AI is being implemented and used increasingly. Therefore, a technical understanding of 
AI may have a significant influence on the effectiveness of its use. Despite related stud-
ies on AI and its role in education, research on the relationship between AI technical 
understanding and students’ AI self-efficacy is still lacking. Considering that, the follow-
ing hypotheses were put forward:

Technical understanding of AI significantly predicts AI self-efficacy.
Technical understanding of AI significantly predicts AI output quality.
Technical understanding of AI significantly predicts students’ academic perfor-
mance.

AI practical application
Educational systems should teach students how to use AI tools meaningfully, ethically 
and effectively (Bećirović & Mattoš, 2024; Stracke et al., 2025). Ng et al. (Ng et al., 2021b) 
emphasized that “applying AI knowledge, concepts, and applications in different scenar-
ios” constitutes one of the four pillars of AI literacy (p. 507). Students generally exhibit a 
positive attitude towards AI tools such as ChatGPT (Haque et al., 2022) and are mostly 
in favor of applying such tools into education as these can support the learning journey 
(Kamoun et al., 2024; Lepik, 2024; Mohamed et al., 2024).

Most notably, AI tools are used to gather information as they can effectively and effi-
ciently retrieve vast amounts of data in a structured and personalized form (Asirit & 
Hua, 2023; Mohamed et al., 2024; Potter et al., 2024). AI tools have also been employed 
successfully to improve students’ writing skills (Pratama & Hastuti, 2024), helping them 
overcome writer’s block by providing prompts and fostering creativity (Lepik, 2024; 
Shanto et  al., 2024) and obtaining feedback on a text (Brew et  al., 2023; Potter et  al., 
2024). Similarly, AI technologies have been used in online assessment and students 
appreciate the immediate feedback and the opportunity to follow their progress while 
enjoying the gamification aspect provided (Ningsih, 2023). In another vein, student-AI 
collaboration has been shown to positively impact creativity in the context of drawing, 
depending on students’ AI skills and attitudes (Kim & Lee, 2023). Consequently, we 
wanted to evaluate AI’s practical application on students’ AI self-efficacy, output quality, 
and academic performance and proposed the following hypotheses:

Practical application of AI significantly predicts AI self-efficacy.

Practical application of AI significantly predicts students’ academic performance.

AI critical appraisal
In order to be able to use AI technology safely and ethically, students must be able 
to use their “higher-order thinking skills” (Ng et  al., 2021a) to reflect critically on 
various issues connected to AI tools and their’ output. Critical thinking refers to the 
ability to “methodically review and interpret information in a logical manner, ana-
lyze underlying assumptions, evaluate the credibility of evidence, and ultimately 
reach well-founded and valid conclusions” (Shanto et  al., 2024). Assessing the use 
of AI technology is critical to ensuring that its employment is ethically sound in the 
given circumstances. Also, it is essential for students to evaluate AI carefully in order 
to communicate and collaborate responsibly with this complex technology (Long & 
Magerko, 2020) and take issues of data protection and privacy into account (Asirit & 
Hua, 2023).

The importance of acquiring AI skills to remain competitive in the job market and 
a potential over-reliance on technology to the detriment of human interaction are 
topics that cause concern. Likewise, students are aware that output generated by AI 
might not be entirely reliable and be subject to certain hidden biases (Asirit & Hua, 
2023; Kamoun et  al., 2024; Lepik, 2024). This was confirmed by Austrian respond-
ents who expressed concerns about the reliability of data accessed by AI (Pricewater-
houseCoopers, 2023b), which concurs with research that has shown that AI-driven 
conversational models struggle with reliability and bias issues (Amaro et  al., 2023; 
Brew et al., 2023). Some also consider AI-based technology to inhibit critical thinking 
and a tool that makes the learning experience more passive (Potter et  al., 2024). AI 
tools have also been labeled as applications that might encourage plagiarism in aca-
demic writing, especially among weaker students, echoing persistent concerns among 
educational institutions (Aithal & Aithal, 2023; Shanto et al., 2024).

Recent findings show that, while using AI to support the creation of high-quality 
output, students do recognize the importance of engaging with the generated text to 
check its plausibility and applicability to the task at hand (Lepik, 2024). Similarly, in 
the Austrian context, younger people are also more concerned with the accuracy of 
the output generated by AI-based systems such as ChatGPT (PricewaterhouseCoop-
ers, 2023b). Therefore, the wish to exclude AI from the educational sector has been 
voiced and may be connected to concerns about an overreliance on these tools and 
the dreaded negative impact on skills such as critical thinking, independent work 
(Potter et  al., 2024), creativity, and the quality of generated output. Accordingly, in 
our study, we wanted to assess the impact of students’ AI critical appraisal on their AI 
self-efficacy, output quality, and academic performance. In order to achieve this aim, 
we hypothesized:

Critical appraisal of AI significantly predicts AI self-efficacy.
Critical appraisal of AI significantly predicts AI output quality.

AI Output quality
According to TAM (the technology acceptance model), people will focus on “how well 
the system performs those tasks” (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000, p. 191) that are relevant 
to reaching the desired outcome. This is supported by findings that students perceive 
ChatGPT to improve the quality of their knowledge, possibly also through the reading 
of AI-generated academic texts (Kamoun et  al., 2024). Further, it has been found that 
ChatGPT was able to provide students with feedback on assignments, asking thought-
provoking questions to encourage students to think more deeply about certain concepts 
and, thereby, enhancing critical thinking and output quality (Shanto et al., 2024). How-
ever, in other cases, the output generated by AI tools has been labeled as frequently con-
taining generic thoughts and repetitive argumentation (Lepik, 2024).

Students in Estonia have been found to employ AI tools to escape overwork and reduce 
the workload placed on them by their studies. In these cases, AI is not perceived as an 
‘easy way out’ but as a welcome support to generate ideas or, in some cases, entire texts. 
However, students are critical of AI output quality and have pointed out that it must be 
treated with care, checked, and verified (Lepik, 2024). Particularly, the issue of wrong or 
non-existent references or sources given by AI tools caused a significant amount of skep-
ticism, and students expressed appreciation of scholarly databases as a more reliable and 
easily accessible source of information. Further, an overreliance on AI tools may impact 
student performance negatively (Khoso et al., 2023). Therefore, the following hypothesis 
was proposed:

AI output quality of AI significantly predicts students’ academic performance.

AI self‑efficacy
Self-efficacy generally refers to “the conviction that one can successfully execute the 
behavior required to produce the [desired] outcomes” (Bandura, 1977, p. 193). Chat-
bots and AI have the ability to influence self-efficacy positively (Parsakia, 2023). Studies 
revealed that people who experience success when using technology or, specifically, AI 
feel they are more self-efficacious and, thus, do not have any negative feelings related 
to these tools (Bećirović & Dervić, 2022; Kraus et  al., 2020; Yildiz Durak, 2023). This 
implies, in turn, that the fast-paced development of new AI technologies might inter-
fere with users’ perceived self-efficacy as the demand to continuously improve one’s 
skills exerts considerable pressure. This might lead to a more negative perception of AI 
and cause individuals stress. Both, increased pressure and stress could contribute to 
decreased self-efficacy (Ahmetović et al., 2020; Bandura et al., 1999). Further, experienc-
ing anxiety when using technology can result in less interest in employing such tools for 
educational purposes (Wang et al., 2023).

Apart from higher demands placed on skills development, acceptance of technology 
can be affected by aspects such as user-friendliness, gamification and perceived individ-
ual digital competence, as well as by the subjectively attributed usefulness of the technol-
ogy concerned (Venkatesh & Morris, 2000). However, if competence has been increased 



Bećirović et al. Smart Learning Environments           (2025) 12:29 Page 8 of 25

through technology, this can increase intrinsic motivation (Dautbašić & Bećirović, 2022; 
Schweder & Raufelder, 2021), which is in accordance with the above claim that success-
ful usage of AI increases the perception of self-efficacy (Kraus et al., 2020; Yildiz Durak, 
2023). Accordingly, the following hypotheses were proposed:

AI self-efficacy of AI significantly predicts students’ AI output quality.
AI self-efficacy of AI significantly predicts students’ academic performance.

The age range of the participants varied from 18 to 60 (M = 34.6; SD = 11.3). They were 
categorized into three age groups: 18–29 (N = 113; 39.5%), 30–39 (N = 73; 25.5%), and 
40 + (N = 100; 35.0%). This categorisation depicts the varied age spectrum of students in

as career changes are prominent. Therefore, the research sample encompasses typical 
students and a substantial percentage of adult learners who balance their job commit-
ments with higher education. The first age group represents typical university entry-age 
students, who have completed their secondary education and are pursuing their stud-
ies. The second age group includes older students, as Austria has 18% of students over 
30 years old (Austrian Student Social Survey, 2019). This group represents adults in 
their early to mid-career stages, particularly since Austria has many working students, 
especially in universities of applied sciences (Austrian Student Social Survey, 2019), and 
university colleges of teacher education, who often manage work alongside their further 
education or career changes. The third age category, 40 + , includes students in their 
mid to late career stages, pursuing typically master’s degrees, PhDs, career changes, or 
engaging in further or continuous education.

An array of academic fields is represented by the participants, such as students study-
ing to become primary school teachers (N = 104; 36.4%), students in the field of elemen-
tary pedagogy (N = 21; 7.3%), students studying to become secondary school teachers 
(N = 30; 10.5%), students in the field of economics (N = 36; 12.6%), students in the field 
of digital education, (N = 32; 11.2%), as well as students in other pedagogical fields 
(N = 62; 21.7%). In Austria, primary school teachers are responsible for teaching pupils 
aged between six and ten in all subjects except for religious education, while elementary 
pedagogues take care of and educate children from the age of 2.5 years until they enter 
primary school.

The research sample comprised 105 (37.1%) students holding university entrance qual-
ifications, 86 (30.4%) with BA, 81 (28.6%) with MA, 4 (1.4%) with PhDs, and 7 (2.4%) 
with other educational levels. In terms of gender, the study consisted mainly of female 
students (N = 227; 79.4%) outnumbering males (N = 56; 19.6%), with three individuals 
(1%) choosing not to disclose their gender.

Data analysis
The data collected during this research were analyzed using the statistical software 
packages SPSS and AMOS, both of which were version 29.0. Following the establish-
ment of the data’s reliability and the distribution’s normality, descriptive results were 
examined. Before performing structural equation modeling (SEM), confirmatory 
factor analysis (CFA) was employed to investigate the fit between observed indica-
tors and underlying constructs, as recommended by Anderson and Gerbing (1988).

Preliminary results
The variables studied had mean values between 2.62 and 5.92, and standard deviations 
between 1.11 to 1.78. (Table 3). The participants scored lowest on AI technical under-
standing (M = 2.62; SD = 1.51), suggesting they needed more comprehension of the tech-
nical aspects of AI technologies. Further, the participants obtained the greatest score in 
academic achievement (M = 5.92; SD = 1.11), implying that AI could be a beneficial and 
valuable tool for their learning. However, students’ AI output quality scores could be 
higher (M = 3.18; SD = 1.78), signaling that they still have issues with AI output and need 
training on using AI tools effectively. The results related to AI self-efficacy (M = 3.12; 
SD = 1.44) confirm students’ relatively low AI technical understanding and need for 
guidance on how to utilize AI tools meaningfully and ethically. Additionally, the descrip-
tive data shows that the responses for AI practical application (M = 4.59; SD = 1.46) and 
AI critical appraisal (M = 4.73; SD = 1.50) were primarily positive, indicating a tendency 
to apply AI tools actively and critically in their learning processes.

The Pearson correlation analysis (Table  3) demonstrates the significant association 
between almost all relations among the investigated constructs. The highest correlation 
was obtained between AI critical appraisal and AI practical application (r = 0.803), indi-
cating students’ high consideration for the proper and adequate use of AI tools. High 
and significant correlations were obtained between AI technical understanding and AI 
practical application (r = 0.622), demonstrating the increase of AI tools use as students 
increase their technical understanding of these tools. Interestingly, there was no rela-
tionship between AI output quality and academic achievement (r = 0.041). As for the 
normality distribution (Table 3), all variables exhibited skewness and kurtosis values fall-
ing within the permitted ranges of -3 to + 3 and -10 to + 10, correspondingly, having val-
ues spanning a range from -1.583 to 1.002 and -0.876 to 3.576 (Brown, 2006).

Testing the structural model
The structural model was determined to have a good model fit (χ2 = 1459,788, χ2 
/df = 1,653, TLI = 0.946, CFI = 0.952, SRMR = ,0525, RMSEA = 0.048, PCLOSE 0,787. 
Six of the twelve hypotheses (Table  6) were confirmed (H1, H2, H3, H5, H6, and 
H7), whereas six were refuted (H4, H8, H9, H10, H11, and H12). AI technical under-
standing significantly predicted (β = 0.441, p < 0.001) AI self-efficacy (H1) but not 
(β = 0.052, p = 0.572) AI output quality (H4) and academic performance (β = -0,158, 
p = 0.96) (H8). While AI critical appraisal was a significant predictor (β = -0. 348, 
p = 0.016) of AI self-efficacy (H2) and AI output quality (β = -0. 571, p = 0.002) (H5), 
there was an insignificant association between AI critical appraisal and academic 
performance (β = 0.343, p = 0.81) (H9). Further, the practical application of AI had a 
significant impact (β = 0.705, p < 0.001) on AI self-efficacy (H3) and AI output qual-
ity (β = 0.599, p = 0.005) (H6). However, AI practical application was an insignificant 
predictor (β = 0.035, p = 0.874) of academic performance (H10). In addition, AI self-
efficacy significantly (β = 0.229, p = 0.029) predicted AI output quality (H7) but not 
academic performance (β = 0.116, p = 0.271) (H11). Further, AI output quality was 
also an insignificant predictor (β = 0.004, p = 0.955) of academic performance (H12).

Discussion
This study aimed to investigate the effect of Austrian students’ AI literacy on their 
AI self-efficacy, output quality, and academic performance. In particular, it sought 
to determine the influence of AI technical understanding, critical appraisal, practi-
cal application, self-efficacy, and output quality on students’ academic achievement 
as well as the effects of students’ AI output quality, and AI self-efficacy on their aca-
demic performance. The following subsections discuss the findings in light of the 
higher education context and previous research. However, due to the fact that AI lit-
eracy is a relatively new and emerging area of research in higher education, and the 
lack of research exploring all of the relationships included in our research design, in 
some cases, we have compared our results with similar previous studies.

Supported relationships
The results of the structural model presented in Table 6 show that the following rela-
tionships are significant: TU → SE (,441), CA → SE (−,348), PA → SE (,705), CA → OQ 
(−,571), PA → OQ (,599), SE → OQ (,229).

The results indicate that a technical understanding of AI significantly predicts AI self-
efficacy (H1). This suggests that students who have a deep understanding of the key con-
cepts and functions of AI, as well as the ability to evaluate its limitations, opportunities, 
advantages, and disadvantages and to envision new applications for AI, rely more heavily 
on their skills and abilities in challenging situations and when tackling complex tasks in 
contrast to those with a limited understanding of AI technology. These results are sup-
ported by Stolpe and Hallström (2024), who found an association between the interest 
in and understanding of technology and self-efficacy on a meta-level, as well as by Ayan-
wale et  al. (2024), who revealed that a thorough understanding of AI indirectly influ-
ences the ability to solve problems with AI effectively.

The students’ critical appraisal of AI tools had a significant negative impact on their AI 
self-efficacy (H2). This suggests that when students analytically assess AI tools, includ-
ing privacy, security, legal issues, risks, ethical considerations, weaknesses, and soci-
etal and individual impacts, they may feel less confident in using AI to solve problems, 
find solutions, and be productive. Our results are supported by Obenza et  al. (2024), 
who revealed a mediating effect of trust in AI on students’ self-efficacy. Other studies 
(Choung et al., 2023; Kraus et al., 2020; Montag et al., 2023; Schepman & Rodway, 2023) 
found a positive association between students’ affirmative view of AI and their self-effi-
cacy. These results further suggest that excessive doubts and uncertainties regarding AI 
output have a detrimental effect on students’ self-efficacy. This implies that in order for 
students to feel more confident in using AI technology, their confidence in AI output 
and their capabilities must be strengthened.

The results of our study also demonstrate a significant negative impact of critical 
appraisal on output quality (H5), which reinforces the findings and supports the inter-
pretation of the previous hypothesis. Additionally, these findings indicate that students 
have unfulfilled expectations regarding the quality, efficiency, and efficacy of AI tools in 
carrying out certain tasks, primarily due to concerns about ethical, security, privacy and 
risk factors associated with AI and its outcomes. Previous studies (Amaro et al., 2023; 
Asirit & Hua, 2023; Kamoun et al., 2024; Lepik, 2024; PricewaterhouseCoopers, 2023b; 
Shanto et al., 2024) support our results and their interpretation by suggesting that there 
is a relationship between students’ awareness that AI technology needs to be critically 
reflected and assessed and the quality of AI output.

Consistent with previous studies, (Essel et al., 2022; Kraus et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2022; 
Yildiz Durak, 2023) practical application of AI significantly predicted AI self-efficacy 
(H3), revealing that being able to use AI technologies for daily professional or personal 
use, being aware of the importance of AI and familiar with apps that have integrated AI 
into their systems, assessing if problems should be solved with AI tools, and critically 
evaluating the implications of AI use, significantly affect students’ abilities to apply AI 
tools successfully in solving problems, finding solutions and executing the school tasks. 
These results are in line with Pintrich et al. (1993), who found that mastering tasks is 
influential to students’ self-efficacy and with Bećirović et al. (2022), who revealed that 



Bećirović et al. Smart Learning Environments           (2025) 12:29 Page 16 of 25

the time students spend online significantly impacts their internet self-efficacy. There-
fore, as students improve their understanding of and are more familiar and experienced 
with AI technology, their self-efficacy in using AI for personal and professional tasks 
increases significantly.

The practical application of AI significantly predicted output quality (H6), which is in 
line with other studies (Asirit & Hua, 2023; Ng et al., 2021a, 2021b) that prove that uti-
lizing AI in practice is crucial for successful performance and efficiency. The confirma-
tion of this hypothesis also supports the previous hypothesis’s discussion and inferences. 
Thus, daily use of AI for personal or professional needs, familiarity with technologies 
that integrate AI into their operations, the ability to solve problems by using AI, and 
the awareness of AI and its critical evaluation significantly influence students’ AI output 
quality.

The results show that AI self-efficacy significantly predicts the quality of AI output 
(H7). This suggests that students’ abilities to handle and solve problems, perform com-
plex tasks, and continuously learn and stay updated using new AI tools, significantly 
impact the quality, ease, effectiveness, and productivity of their learning. These results 
align with previous related studies that found self-efficacy to be a significant predictor 
of students’ motivation, learning, choices, effort, persistence (Zimmerman, 2000), and 
performance (Bernacki et al., 2015).

Unsupported relationships
The results (Table 6) show that the following relationships are insignificant: TU → OQ 
(,052), TU → AP (−,158), CA → AP (,343), PA → AP (,035), SE → AP (,116), OQ → AP 
(,004).

The findings indicated that there is no significant relationship between technical 
understanding of AI and AI output quality (H4). This suggests that students’ familiarity 
with AI systems, concepts, and machine and deep learning models does not significantly 
impact their ease, quality, effectiveness, and efficiency of learning by employing AI tools. 
The AI output quality scores of our participants are rather low, indicating that they per-
ceive AI to improve the quality, efficiency, effectiveness, and productivity of their learn-
ing only to a limited extent. Our results corroborate the findings of Lepik (Lepik, 2024), 
whose participants express concerns regarding AI output in terms of accuracy, authen-
ticity, and validity. Furthermore, the fear of being insufficiently prepared to use AI out-
put adequately (Kamoun et al., 2024) or the concern of limited creativity of AI output 
(Lepik, 2024; Pavlik, 2023) may explain the participants’ low scores in AI output. The 
results are also in line with related studies conducted by Bećirović (2023b), indicating 
that the use of LMS platforms does not significantly influence output quality.

Contrary to Ayanwale et  al. (2024), there was an insignificant influence of technical 
understanding of AI on students’ academic performance (H8). Overall, our participants 
reported a low level of technical understanding of AI technology while achieving high 
scores in academic performance. The lack of AI technical understanding may account 
for its insignificant impact on students’ academic achievement. The current results are 
particularly noteworthy when compared to the findings of Asirit and Hua (2023), Chu 
et  al. (2022), Kamoun et  al. (2024a), and Richter et  al., (Zawacki-Richter et  al., 2019) 
whose research indicates that students’ technical understanding is largely influenced by 



B	 ećirović et al. Smart Learning Environments           (2025) 12:29 Page 17 of 25

their field of study. Therefore, our findings may be explained by the fact that most par-
ticipants come from fields, in Austria, generally associated with minimal technological 
affinity (Brandhofer & Micheuz, 2011; Brandhofer et al., 2019), specifically elementary, 
primary, and secondary education preservice teachers, whose knowledge of systems, 
concepts, and models of machine and deep learning does not significantly impact their 
academic achievement. In contrast to our findings, Asirit and Hua (2023a) discovered 
that students in educational disciplines show elevated levels of technological compre-
hension. Additionally, Brandhofer and Tengler (2024) reported in their investigation 
on AI acceptance among university students and educators that 56.2% of participants 
claimed to possess the requisite knowledge for competent AI application. This suggests 
that our participants need adequate and sufficient training to cultivate understanding, 
knowledge, skills, and competencies to effectively utilize AI technology in educational 
settings to enhance the effects of AI use on their academic performance.

In contrast to Chan and Hu (2023), whose findings suggest that students’ concerns 
regarding AI may promote their learning results, our findings revealed that critical 
appraisal of AI had no significant effect on students’ academic performance (H9). This 
suggests that the students’ concerns regarding AI use such as ethical, privacy, security, 
risks, legal issues, etc. do not significantly influence their academic performance, nei-
ther positively nor negatively. Further, this indicates students’ insecurity with AI use for 
academic purposes and their commitment to follow ethical and legal standards in their 
learning and while doing their university assignments. Moreover, the immediate accessi-
bility of rapid responses may diminish students’ engagement with the subject matter and 
consequently impede their learning (Cotton et al., 2023; Sallam et al., 2023). The findings 
of Zastudil et al. (2023), which demonstrate that AI can promote critical thinking and 
improve learning outcomes, along with Dahri et al. (2024), which reveal that AI-based 
academic support significantly enhances students’ academic achievement, indicate 
that students necessitate adequate training and support for the effective utilization of 
AI-powered tools in their educational pursuits. Consequently, AI proficiency among 
educators and students is essential and the integration of AI in teaching and learning 
should represent a pedagogical revolution rather than merely a technological transition 
(Grájeda et al., 2024).

Contrary to Lee et al. (2022), as well as to García-Martínez et al. (2023), and in line 
with Maamor et al. (2024) the impact of AI practical application on students’ academic 
performance (H10) was insignificant, suggesting that students’ current knowledge, skills, 
experiences, and abilities of AI use for academic purposes do not significantly affect 
their academic achievement. These results may be further explained by the fact that stu-
dents use AI for academic purposes with wariness (Fotea et al., 2019), as the concern is 
that the practical application of AI is associated with wrong information, and the inhibi-
tion of critical thinking and cheating, which is still prevalent (Kamoun et al., 2024; Price-
waterhouseCoopers, 2023b). These arguments could be further substantiated by Wecks 
et al. (2024), who discovered that students’ utilization of GenAI adversely affects their 
exam performance. This occurs as students employ GenAI tools as a means to circum-
vent workload, thereby evading the cognitive processes of comprehension, analysis, and 
summarization, which hinders their retention of learning materials and impedes their 
deeper understanding (Wecks et al., 2024). Further, the influence of AI output quality 



Bećirović et al. Smart Learning Environments           (2025) 12:29 Page 18 of 25

on students’ academic performance (H12) was insignificant, implying that the effective-
ness, efficacy and quality of their learning by using AI technology does not significantly 
influence their academic performance. The experiences of our participants may be simi-
lar those of University of Auckland students, who strongly oppose the implementation 
of AI tutors, asserting that “AI is constantly incorrect, environmentally damaging and 
such a stupid way of learning” and “How am I supposed to reliably learn topics for a 
test when the AI will barely know what it’s saying and spew out incorrect and irrelevant 
information?” (Raphael & Plummer, 2025, para. 4). Additionally, our results are in line 
with previous studies, which indicated that students are not convinced that AI output 
corresponds to the quality standards necessary to achieve the targeted results (Asirit & 
Hua, 2023). Furthermore, our results are related to the findings of Wecks et al. (2024), 
who discovered that while using GenAI increases student engagement, it actually hin-
ders learning and diminishes learning outcomes. Consequently, in order to improve the 
impact of AI use on students’ performance, both the use of AI strategies and the quality 
and accuracy of AI-powered products must be enhanced.

According to a meta-analysis of 51 studies conducted by Honicke and Broadbent 
(2016), academic performance and academic self-efficacy have a moderate positive cor-
relation with goal orientation, deep processing techniques and effort control, acting 
as mediating and moderating variables. In consonance with related studies (Baruah & 
Sabu, 2024; Bećirović et al., 2022) on the impact of students’ self-efficacy on their learn-
ing outcomes, our study showed a positive but insignificant relationship between AI 
self-efficacy and academic performance (H11). The generally low AI self-efficacy results 
imply that students perceive their skills in keeping up to date with the rapid develop-
ment in the field of AI, as well as using AI in difficult situations and solving complicated 
tasks, to be limited. Further, such results may also stem from the improper utilization of 
AI tools, as students often employ them as shortcuts to get outcomes instead of actively 
and deeply engaging in reading, analyzing, synthesizing, problem-solving, and creating 
during the learning process. These results further underscore the necessity for enhanc-
ing AI utilization skills and the quality of AI tools to properly harness AI’s potential.

Conclusion
The findings of this study indicate that technical understanding of AI significantly and 
positively influenced AI self-efficacy, which in turn significantly and positively affected 
AI output quality. Given that participants demonstrated the lowest levels of comprehen-
sion in AI technology, it is crucial to enhance students’ AI technological knowledge and 
awareness. These skills are essential for effectively and confidently deploying AI, which 
in turn should positively impact their academic achievement.

The current study also revealed that the practical application of AI had a signifi-
cant and positive influence on AI self-efficacy and AI output quality, while AI critical 
appraisal significantly and negatively impacted AI self-efficacy and AI output quality. 
These findings highlight the importance of comprehending the utilization of AI and 
thoughtfully implementing AI technology. Additionally, they suggest that students pos-
sess a strong awareness of the potential problems associated with the employment of 
AI. At the same time, a lack of AI technical understanding, as well as an unsubstantiated 



B	 ećirović et al. Smart Learning Environments           (2025) 12:29 Page 19 of 25

hypercritical approach, may lead to an unfavorable perception of AI and, thus, create a 
feeling of incompetence and poor results when using AI.

Although technical understanding of AI significantly influenced AI self-efficacy, its 
impact on AI output quality and academic performance was insignificant, implying 
that the effectiveness of AI use still needs to be improved among Austrian university 
students. Further, AI critical appraisal, practical application, self-efficacy, and output 
quality had an insignificant effect on academic performance, implying the necessity of 
comprehensively training and improving students’ AI skills to enhance academic perfor-
mance subsequently.

The findings emphasize the importance of enhancing AI literacy among students by 
improving their comprehension, awareness, skills, knowledge, and competencies; inte-
grating AI applications into the curricula; and addressing potential risks to ensure the 
effective, efficient, ethical, and legal use of AI-powered tools to improve students’ aca-
demic performance through the usage of AI tools.

Limitations and suggestions for further research
Regardless of the detailed design of this study and its value, there are certain limitations 
and proposals for further research. Although distributing an online questionnaire has 
advantages, such as the relatively quick and direct access to the target population, the 
readiness to participate remains limited (Lefever et  al., 2007). It cannot be ruled out 
that participants provide socially desirable responses rather than express their actual 
perspectives (Van de Mortel, 2008). The sample did not include teachers at universities, 
which is why their AI literacy and its impact on students’ academic outcomes remains 
open. In addition, the study’s cross-sectional design did not allow exploration of AI lit-
eracy development over time as students gain experience and enhance their skills.

Future research could address these limitations by conducting quasi-experimental 
studies comparing the academic outcomes of students using AI with those of students 
not using AI, which could provide insight into the specific impact of AI applications on 
different learning scenarios and study fields. Further, investigating university lecturers’ 
AI literacy may shed light on its influence on students’ academic performance. Moreo-
ver, longitudinal studies could offer insights into the development of AI literacy as well as 
its influence on and relationship with other factors determining academic performance.

Study contributions and practical implications
This study contributes to the professional literature on the impact of students’ AI literacy 
on their academic performance. Its relevance stems from the potential practical implica-
tions in legal, institutional, and personnel terms that may help policymakers, curricula 
developers, and teachers improve students’ AI literacy and, consequently, increase their 
educational outcomes. Likewise, as AI increasingly influences the educational settings, 
universities, instructors, administrators, and students must accept, adapt to, and effec-
tively and ethically utilize AI-powered tools. Similarly, the integration of AI-powered 
tools into educational practices should be regarded not merely as an add-on or tech-
nological progress but as a transformative pedagogical revolution necessitating critical 
comprehension, awareness, knowledge, skills and attitudes to equip students with the 
competencies essential for effective engagement in an AI-driven society.



Bećirović et al. Smart Learning Environments           (2025) 12:29 Page 20 of 25

As results showed that AI technical understanding and practical application had a 
significantly positive influence on AI self-efficacy, and AI practical application and AI 
self-efficacy positively and significantly impacted AI output quality, it seems pertinent 
to develop or adapt curricula that integrate AI literacy in university courses in order to 
strengthen students’ theoretical and practical knowledge in AI-powered tools applica-
tion. University policies and guidelines should, therefore, not focus on preventing the 
use of AI but rather on providing a framework for the safe, ethical and effective use of AI 
in academic contexts.

Considering that AI critical appraisal significantly and negatively influences AI self-
efficacy and output quality, teachers should integrate AI technology into their teaching 
by addressing the opportunities, risks, and concerns while providing learning experi-
ences that include the proper use of AI-powered tools. Consequently, further educa-
tion and training programs for teachers must be adapted or developed to enable them 
to integrate AI knowledge and applications in a technically, didactically, legally, and ethi-
cally sound manner, thereby promoting the effective implementation of AI technology in 
the education system.

Previous research indicated both positive and negative impacts of AI utilization on 
students’ academic achievement. Since none of our five tested variables (AI technical 
understanding, AI critical appraisal, AI practical application, AI self-efficacy, and AI 
output quality) significantly affected students’ academic achievement, either positively 
or negatively, educational institutions should reconsider integrating AI into teaching 
and learning methodologies. Reevaluating university policies on AI use and assessment 
should be the initial step toward enhancing students’ academic performance through 
AI. This process must ensure that AI-generated outputs do not replace students’ essen-
tial academic skills such as reasoning, critical thinking, writing, problem-solving, and 
research. Additionally, students’ training should not focus solely on developing their 
technical AI skills; rather, it should strategically support learning by emphasizing the 
teaching and learning context while prioritizing pedagogical effectiveness and improv-
ing their academic achievement. Consequently, AI tools should function as aids rather 
than replacements for students’ cognitive processes during educational experiences. 
This approach must also consider awareness and build positive attitudes and percep-
tions among students to use AI tools meaningfully and efficiently. Likewise, instructors 
should effectively guide students through examples of employing AI tools for creativity, 
research, problem-solving, writing, and critical thinking development and enhancement 
instead of simply enabling them to generate AI output, which could significantly harm 
their academic performance.
